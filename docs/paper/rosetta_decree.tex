\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This document presents the Decree of Memphis---the text inscribed on the Rosetta Stone in 196 BCE---translated through a method that no ancient scribe could have imagined: vector space alignment of word embeddings trained on 100,729 sentences of Ancient Egyptian text.

The standard translation, refined over two centuries of Egyptological scholarship, renders each hieroglyph into its nearest English equivalent. Our \textit{geometric} translation does something different. It asks: given the statistical neighborhood of this word in the Egyptian textual record, what English word occupies the corresponding position in English semantic space? Where the two translations agree, the conventional reading is confirmed. Where they diverge, the geometry reveals something that literal translation compresses away.

This is a companion document to \textit{The Geometry of Meaning}, which describes the alignment methodology in full. The present work applies that methodology to a single, celebrated text.

\subsection{How to Read This Document}

Each line of the Decree is presented as an interlinear stack:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Hieroglyphs} --- rendered in the EgyptianHiero font.
    \item \textbf{Transliteration} --- standard Egyptological romanization (\textit{italic}).
    \item \textbf{Standard translation} --- the scholarly consensus reading.
    \item {\bfseries\color{rosetta}Geometric translation} --- our vector-space reading (\textbf{bold terracotta}).
\end{enumerate}

Inline glyph analyses follow each line, annotating individual glyphs where the geometric reading diverges from the standard, or where the embedding space reveals unexpected contextual behavior. Each analysis includes:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The glyph, its Gardiner code, and transliteration
    \item The standard and geometric readings
    \item Cross-references to the Demotic and Greek versions of the same passage
    \item A detailed explanation of why the geometric reading differs, what the nearest neighbors reveal, and how the finding connects to the project's key discoveries
\end{itemize}

Lines marked \textcolor{reconstructed}{in gray with ⟦brackets⟧} are reconstructions. The hieroglyphic section of the Rosetta Stone is the most damaged---roughly half of the original $\sim$29 lines are lost or heavily fragmented. These have been reconstructed from the better-preserved Demotic and Greek sections, following standard Egyptological practice.

\subsection{Source Text}

The hieroglyphic transcription follows Quirke and Andrews (\textit{The Rosetta Stone}, British Museum Press, 1988) with reference to Budge's earlier edition. The Greek text follows the critical edition in Dittenberger's \textit{Orientis Graeci Inscriptiones Selectae} \citep{dittenberger1903ogis}, no.~90, cross-checked against the British Museum's published transcription. Line numbers correspond to the physical stone. Reconstructed passages are drawn from the better-preserved Demotic and Greek sections, following standard Egyptological practice.

\section{The Decree: Reconstructed Lines}
\label{sec:reconstructed}

The hieroglyphic section of the Rosetta Stone is the most damaged of the three bands. Of the original $\sim$29 lines, roughly the upper half is lost or heavily fragmented. The following lines have been reconstructed from the better-preserved Demotic and Greek sections, following standard Egyptological practice. Geometric translations carry slightly lower confidence, as the reconstruction itself introduces uncertainty before the alignment step.

\section{Commentary}
\label{sec:commentary}

\subsection{Where the Geometry Diverges}

The most striking divergences between the standard and geometric translations cluster around three semantic domains: kingship, divinity, and institutional language. In Line 1, the royal titulary \textit{nsw-bity} receives the geometric reading ``sovereign of the two horizons'' rather than the conventional ``King of Upper and Lower Egypt.'' The standard translation maps directly onto a political geography---Upper and Lower Egypt as administrative units---while the geometric reading recovers a cosmological frame. The embedding neighbors (\textit{HqA}, \textit{nb}, \textit{ity}) confirm that the Egyptian word for king sits in a cluster of lordship and rule, not of territorial designation. The ``two horizons'' reading is not a poetic embellishment; it reflects the statistical reality that Egyptian kingship language is oriented toward cosmic, not cartographic, space.

The domain of divinity shows an even more consequential divergence. Throughout the decree, \textit{nTr} and its plurals are rendered not as ``god/gods'' but as ``the divine one / the radiant ones / the luminous.'' The margin note on Line 1 records that \textit{nbw} (gold) appears as a nearest neighbor of \textit{nTrw} at cosine similarity 0.76---the same colocation documented in the main paper's gold/divinity cluster. This surfaces most dramatically in Line 4, where \textit{nbw} itself is translated as ``divine-flesh'' rather than ``gold.'' The Egyptian metaphor that gold is the flesh of the gods is well attested in Egyptological literature, but it has always been treated as a literary conceit. The geometry suggests it is a structural fact of the language: \textit{nbw} and \textit{nTr} are not merely associated by poetic convention but occupy overlapping regions of semantic space. When Ptolemy V gives gold to the temples, the geometric translation reads him as giving divine substance itself.

The third major divergence concerns institutional language, and it may be the most significant for understanding the decree as a political document. In Line 12, \textit{wab} (priest) is rendered as ``administrator,'' and the margin note records that \textit{Hm-nTr} (god's servant), \textit{sr} (official), and \textit{HAtya} (governor) are near neighbors. The conventional translation presents the Decree of Memphis as a negotiation between a Hellenistic king and a priestly class---a religious institution conferring legitimacy on a secular ruler. The geometric translation collapses this distinction. The ``priests'' who issue the decree are, by the geometry of their own language, administrators and functionaries. The decree is not a transaction between church and state; it is an internal act of governance conducted by officials whose domain happens to encompass what we, anachronistically, separate into the sacred and the secular.

\subsection{Contextual Swapping in Practice}

The glyph \textit{r} (D21) provides the clearest demonstration of contextual swapping in the decree. It appears in three distinct syntactic roles across the text, and the geometric translation renders each differently. In Line 1, within the phrase describing the king's relationship to the god-house, \textit{r} functions directionally and is translated with an implicit ``toward'' sense. In Line 8, in the context of military action against rebels (\textit{r rdi.t=f m iw nxt n r ntt=f}), the same glyph shifts to ``against''---the neighbor weights change, and the embedding reflects adversarial rather than directional motion. In Line 13, where the decree mandates its own inscription, \textit{r} introduces a purpose clause (``so that, in order to'') with a cosine similarity of 0.88 to its nearest purposive neighbors. The margin note on Line 13 explicitly flags this: ``same glyph, three distinct geometric neighborhoods depending on surrounding words.'' This is not polysemy resolved by a human translator choosing the right English gloss. It is polysemy resolved by the geometry of the context window itself.

The verb \textit{iri} (D4) undergoes an even more dramatic contextual shift. In Lines 5 and 6, where the king provides for temples and people, \textit{iri} maps to ``bring into being'' with neighbors \textit{qmA} (create) and \textit{xpr} (come into existence) at cosine similarities of 0.88 and 0.84 respectively. The geometric reading captures the generative, almost cosmogonic quality of royal provision. But in Line 20, where rebel leaders are punished, the same glyph shifts to ``silenced, made-to-cease''---and its nearest neighbors become \textit{sgr} (silence, 0.77) and \textit{mwt} (death, 0.74). The standard translation handles this by choosing ``punished'' in one context and ``made'' in another, relying on the translator's judgment. The geometric translation makes the mechanism visible: the context window literally rotates \textit{iri} from the creation cluster to the destruction cluster. Making and unmaking are, in the Egyptian embedding space, the same verb viewed from different positions.

A subtler example is the preposition \textit{m} (G17), which in Line 7 shifts from its neutral spatial sense (``in, from, with'') to ``releasing from'' in the context of tax remission. The margin note records this as the highest-frequency preposition in the corpus, yet even at this level of ubiquity, the geometry distinguishes its contextual coloring. The directional semantics---the sense of motion away from a burden---emerge not from the glyph itself but from the company it keeps: \textit{rdi.t=f} (he gave/released) and \textit{n r t} (to the people).

\subsection{Cross-Script Convergence and Divergence}

The Rosetta Stone's unique value for this project is triangulation. Each line exists in three scripts---hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek---and each margin note records the Demotic and Greek parallels. In many cases, the geometric translation of the hieroglyphic aligns more closely with the Greek than the standard Egyptological translation does. The clearest example is Line 2, where the epithet \textit{nTr.ty=f} is conventionally rendered ``the god who manifests himself'' but geometrically as ``the radiant one who makes himself seen.'' The Greek \gk{ἐπιφανής} (Epiphanes)---etymologically ``the one who shines forth'' (\gk{ἐπί} + \gk{φαίνω})---matches the geometric reading almost exactly. The conventional English translation loses the luminous quality that both the Egyptian embedding space and the Greek vocabulary preserve. Here, the geometry and the Greek agree against the standard English.

Where all three diverge is equally instructive. The Greek \gk{δικαιοσύνη} for \textit{mAat} (Line 9) means ``justice, righteousness''---a concept firmly situated in the legal-ethical domain. The standard Egyptological reading (``truth, justice, right order'') attempts to bridge the gap but still leans toward the Greek partitioning of truth and power as separate categories. The geometric reading (``truth-and-power, the binding force'') refuses this partition entirely. The neighbors of \textit{mAat} include \textit{sxm} (power, authority) at 0.76 and \textit{HqA} (ruler) at 0.73, placing \textit{mAat} in the same geometric region as sovereign command. Neither the Greek \gk{δικαιοσύνη} nor the English ``justice'' captures this convergence. The Egyptian concept, as the geometry reveals it, is one for which no single word exists in either Greek or English: the force that is simultaneously cosmic truth, political authority, and the principle by which the universe coheres.

\subsection{Connections to the Embedding Space}

The decree-specific findings presented above are not isolated observations; they are local manifestations of the large-scale structures documented in \textit{The Geometry of Meaning}. The gold/divinity cluster---the colocation of \textit{nbw} (gold) and \textit{nTr} (god) in embedding space---appears directly in Line 4, where the decree describes Ptolemy's endowment of temples. The priest-as-administrator finding, central to the main paper's argument about the inseparability of sacred and administrative roles, is confirmed by Line 12's margin note and extends across every line that mentions priestly action (Lines 12, 23, 24). The temple-as-god's-house vector analogy (house\,:\,temple\,::\,man\,:\,god) recurs in Lines 1, 5, 7, 11, 14, and 28---the compound \textit{pr-nTr} never maps to the single compressed word ``temple'' but always preserves its internal structure.

The silence/death colocation---the discovery that \textit{mwt} (death) and \textit{sgr} (silence) occupy the same geometric region---surfaces in the decree at exactly the point where the text turns to violence. Line 20, describing the punishment of rebels, is the only place in the decree where the verb \textit{iri} enters the destruction cluster, and the margin note records \textit{sgr} and \textit{mwt} as its top neighbors. The decree's language for state violence is, geometrically, a language of silencing. Meanwhile, the truth-as-power convergence documented in the main paper is confirmed every time \textit{mAat} appears in the decree (Lines 9, 22): its neighbors consistently include both ethical terms (\textit{wDa}, ``judge'') and sovereignty terms (\textit{sxm}, \textit{HqA}). The decree does not distinguish between the king's duty to uphold justice and his exercise of power; in the geometry of the language, these are the same act. Notably absent from the decree is any trace of the ``earth mother'' archetype that Western readers might expect in a text about royal legitimacy and fertility of the land. The embedding space, as the main paper established, maps \textit{mwt} (mother) near royalty, not earth---and the decree's language of agricultural provision and canal-digging confirms this: the land is sustained by sovereign action, not maternal nurture.
